Every explanation of large language models starts with "it predicts the next
word." That's true, and it's useless. It doesn't tell you why the model
invents a citation, why the same prompt gives a different answer twice, or
why stuffing more documents into the context sometimes makes answers worse.

Four ideas explain nearly all of it.

## 1. It doesn't see words

Text is first cut into **tokens** - fragments that are usually part of a word.
"Engineering" might be one token; an unusual surname might be four. The model
never sees letters, and it never sees words as units.

This is why models are bad at counting letters in a word, why they mangle
rare names, and why non-English text costs more per sentence - languages
underrepresented in the tokeniser get chopped into more pieces. When Tamil or
Sinhala text costs three times the tokens of the equivalent English, that's
not a pricing decision. It's the tokeniser.

## 2. Meaning is a direction

Each token becomes a vector - a long list of numbers. The useful property is
that vectors for related things point in similar directions. "Cardiologist"
sits near "clinician" and far from "carburettor," not because anyone said so,
but because they appear in similar contexts across the training data.

This is the entire basis of semantic search. When I built a book recommender
that matches on *meaning* rather than keywords, all it does is embed your
description, embed every book blurb, and find the nearest vectors. There's no
understanding involved - just geometry over a space where proximity happens
to track meaning.

## 3. Attention decides what matters

A sentence isn't a bag of tokens; order and reference matter. **Attention** is
the mechanism that lets each token look at every other token and weight how
much each one matters to it.

In "the doctor told the patient she was fine," attention is what associates
"she" with one of the two nouns. The model computes, for every token, a
weighted blend of every other token's representation. Stack that operation
dozens of times, each layer refining the last, and you get something that
tracks syntax, reference, tone, and a surprising amount of world knowledge.

Two practical consequences fall out of this:

- **Cost grows faster than input.** Every token attends to every other token,
  so doubling the prompt roughly quadruples the attention work. Long contexts
  aren't just slower - they're disproportionately slower.
- **The middle gets ignored.** Models attend most reliably to the beginning
  and end of a long context. Bury the critical instruction in the middle of
  20,000 tokens and it may as well not be there.

## 4. The output is sampled, not chosen

After all that computation, the model produces a probability distribution over
its entire vocabulary for the next token. Perhaps "the" at 12 percent,
"a" at 9 percent, and a long tail of thousands of others.

Then it **samples** from that distribution. It does not pick the winner.

That single fact explains most of what people find mysterious:

- **The same prompt gives different answers** because you're drawing from a
  distribution. Temperature controls how flat that distribution is - low
  temperature concentrates probability on the top candidates, high temperature
  spreads it out.
- **Hallucination isn't a malfunction.** The model produces the token
  sequence that is *statistically plausible* given the context. A fabricated
  citation is plausible-looking text, which is exactly what the mechanism
  optimises for. There is no separate step where it checks whether the thing
  is true, because there is nothing in the architecture that represents
  truth.
- **Structured output works** by clamping the distribution. When you force a
  model to emit JSON matching a schema, the decoder masks out every token
  that would break the grammar. The model isn't being asked to behave - it's
  being made physically unable to produce invalid output.

## What follows from this

Once the mechanism is clear, most engineering decisions stop being guesswork.

**Retrieval beats fine-tuning for facts.** If the model has no representation
of truth, the fix isn't to train harder - it's to put the correct information
into the context so the plausible continuation is also the correct one.
That's the whole idea behind RAG, and it's why citations matter: they let a
human verify the grounding the model can't verify itself.

**Prompt position is a real variable.** Put instructions and critical facts at
the edges of the context, not buried in the middle.

**Never let the model be the only safeguard.** If a wrong answer has real
consequences, put a deterministic check outside the model. In a clinical
triage system I built, an LLM does the reasoning - but hard-coded rules for
stroke, cardiac, and sepsis presentations override it entirely. The model is
sampling from a distribution. For a suspected heart attack, that is not an
acceptable thing to be doing on its own.

**Cheap wins are usually structural.** Shorter context, tighter schemas, and
smaller models with better retrieval will beat a bigger model on a vague
prompt more often than people expect.

None of this requires understanding the maths of a transformer block. It
requires remembering that the thing on the other end is computing a
probability distribution over fragments of text - and then rolling dice.
