Somebody trains a classifier, reports 85 percent accuracy, and asks whether
that's good. The honest answer is that the question is unanswerable as asked.
Accuracy is a number without units. On one problem 85 percent is excellent;
on another it's worse than doing nothing at all.

Here's the order I work through instead.

## Step 1: Find the number you have to beat

Before any model exists, there is already a baseline: predict the most common
answer, every time. That costs nothing and it is your floor.

I trained an ANN to predict bank-customer churn and got about 85 percent
validation accuracy. Sounds respectable. Then I counted the labels: 2,037 of
10,000 customers had churned. So a model consisting of the single word "no"
scores **79.6 percent**.

The model was worth 5.4 points over a constant. That's the real number, and
it's the one to quote - not because it's flattering, but because anyone
competent will compute it themselves in about ten seconds.

Baselines to check before believing any accuracy figure:

- **Majority class.** Always predict the most frequent label.
- **Random at the observed rate.** Sometimes weaker than majority, sometimes
  the more honest comparison.
- **The dumb heuristic.** One rule a domain expert would write on a napkin.
  For delivery-time prediction, "distance divided by average speed" is a
  brutally effective baseline.
- **Persistence, for anything time-series.** Tomorrow equals today. Most
  next-day price predictors quietly learn exactly this, then present it as a
  beautiful chart of predicted-versus-actual where the two lines hug.

If your model doesn't beat these, you don't have a model. You have a costly
way of computing a constant.

## Step 2: Check whether accuracy is even the right metric

The more skewed your classes, the more accuracy lies.

The standard credit-card fraud dataset is about **0.17 percent** fraudulent.
A model that answers "not fraud" to everything scores **99.83 percent
accuracy** and catches zero fraud. You could put that number on a slide and
nobody in the room would flinch.

This trap has teeth beyond reporting. In one of my own pipelines the *model
selection* step ranked candidates by a score that rewarded exactly this
behaviour - so the pipeline was choosing the most useless model available and
logging a great-looking number next to it. The metric you optimise is the
metric you get.

For imbalanced problems, look at:

- **Precision** - of the cases I flagged, how many were real? This is what
  determines whether people keep trusting the alerts.
- **Recall** - of the real cases, how many did I catch? This is what
  determines what you miss.
- **F1** when you need one number, **PR-AUC** when you need one curve.

Accuracy is fine when classes are roughly balanced and errors cost about the
same in both directions. That describes fewer problems than people assume.

## Step 3: Price the two kinds of error

This is the step that actually answers "how good is good enough," and it has
nothing to do with statistics. It's a question about consequences.

Your model can be wrong in two directions, and they almost never cost the
same:

**Malaria screening from blood smears.** A false negative sends an infected
patient home untreated. A false positive costs one microscopist a few minutes
of confirmation. Those are not comparable. You should be willing to accept a
lot of false positives to move recall up, and the system should be described
as a triage aid that prioritises slides - never as a diagnosis.

**Firearm detection on CCTV.** A missed weapon is catastrophic. But a system
that cries wolf gets muted by the operator in week two, and a muted system has
recall of zero in practice. Here the false-positive cost is *also* severe,
just delayed. That's why I set a deliberately high confidence threshold on
that project - alert fatigue destroys the deployment faster than a missed
low-confidence frame.

**Churn prediction.** A false positive means a retention discount sent to
someone who was staying anyway - a small, known cost. A false negative means a
customer walks. If a discount costs 20 dollars and a lost customer costs 400,
you should be flagging aggressively, and your threshold should be nowhere near
0.5.

Which leads to the step most people skip entirely.

## Step 4: Stop using 0.5

The 0.5 threshold is a default, not a decision. Your model outputs a
probability; *you* choose where to cut it. Once you've priced the two errors,
the threshold follows from the ratio.

If a miss costs twenty times what a false alarm costs, you should be acting on
predictions well below 0.5. Sweep the threshold, plot precision and recall
against it, and pick the point that minimises expected cost. This is usually
worth more than another week of model tuning, and it takes an afternoon.

## Step 5: Ask how much you can trust the number

A metric computed on a small test set is itself uncertain.

I have a malaria classifier that hit about 93 percent validation accuracy -
on **134 validation images**. At that size the 95 percent confidence interval
is roughly plus or minus 4 points. So "93 percent" honestly means "somewhere
between 89 and 97," and the difference between that model and one scoring 90
percent is not measurable with the data I had.

Three habits that keep this honest:

1. **Report the test set size** alongside the metric. Always.
2. **Stratify your splits** so the class balance is the same in train and
   test, and **set a random seed** so the number is reproducible. An
   unstratified split on a 20/80 problem moves your score around by luck
   alone.
3. **Never touch the test set twice.** If you tuned against it, it's a
   validation set now, and your reported figure is optimistic.

## So what's the minimum?

There isn't a universal one. But there is a defensible sentence, and it looks
like this:

> This model needs to exceed 79.6 percent - the majority-class baseline - by
> enough to cover its operating cost, at a threshold tuned so recall stays
> above 80 percent, because a missed churner costs twenty times a wasted
> discount. On a test set of 2,000 it currently reaches that with a margin of
> about 5 points.

That sentence contains a baseline, a metric appropriate to the imbalance, a
cost argument, a threshold justification, and a sample size. Nobody can
meaningfully challenge it without engaging with the actual problem.

"85 percent" contains none of those things - which is why the first question
anyone asks about it is the one you should have answered first.
